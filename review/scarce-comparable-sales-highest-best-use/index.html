When Comparable Sales Are Scarce, Revisit Highest and Best Use

Metrix Realty Group · 4 minute read

On a former-library assignment, the nearest sales pointed toward redevelopment. Metrix’s calls to realtors involved in institutional sales several kilometres away revealed something the local land transactions did not: preschool and child care operators were buying buildings they could put to use.

The question changed from “Where can we find more sales?” to “Are we looking for evidence of the right use?”

That distinction matters for owners, lenders and institutions dealing with unusual properties. A shortage of comparable sales can be a reason to revisit highest and best use before widening the search.

The land case was credible. It still needed testing.

The former library stood on a larger parcel within an urban growth boundary. The surrounding area was in transition, abutting uses supported redevelopment, and several nearby properties had recently been acquired for that purpose.

The initial indication was that value lay in the land. The closest sales reinforced that view.

Metrix tested the redevelopment case using local land sales spread over roughly two years. A density analysis against the Official Plan designation helped establish what the site could support. That work remained part of the investigation.

Separately, Metrix reviewed sales of comparable institutional buildings across the broader market area. These were improved-property transactions, well beyond the immediate neighbourhood.

The realtor calls did the most work

Transaction records supplied prices. Calls to the cooperating realtors established who had bought the institutional buildings and what they had done with them.

The majority of those purchasers were preschool or child care operators, and the buildings had been established for those uses. That buyer pool had been invisible in the nearest redevelopment transactions.

Metrix then reviewed child care provision in the subject’s market area. Supply was limited in the immediate vicinity, and the library’s permitted uses and physical configuration suited that demand.

The most useful comparables were institutional sales several kilometres away. Their relevance came from the utility of the buildings and the purchasers they attracted.

The wider evidence prompted a second look at the initial interpretation of highest and best use. Searching only for nearby land sales would have left that part of the market unexplored.

Scarce comparables can expose a use assumption

Metrix appraiser Roman Virk, P. App., AACI, identifies three considerations in comparable selection: time, location and utility.

Utility is what the property can actually be used for, given its zoning, permitted uses and physical configuration. Two buildings on the same street can have different utility. A building across town may offer a closer functional match.

In the library assignment, proximity favoured redevelopment sales. Investigating utility brought institutional sales into consideration and led to the conversations that explained the buyers’ decisions.

A farther-away transaction still needs analysis of its location and market context. An older sale still needs to be considered against the valuation date. Utility adds a question; it does not erase the others.

This is especially useful when the subject is a former school, church, public building or specialized industrial property, or when the market is small or rural. Simply expanding the search radius can produce more transactions without resolving the assumption behind the search.

Highest and best use must be supported

Highest and best use examines the reasonably probable use that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible and maximally productive. CUSPAP 2026, sections 3.36 and 9.6, calls for consideration of the land as if vacant and the property as improved, with analysis of relevant legal, physical and economic factors.

A shortage of sales does not establish a different highest and best use. Nor does finding a buyer elsewhere prove that the same demand exists at the subject location. Limited local supply, by itself, does not establish financial feasibility.

The library investigation connected those questions: what redevelopment could the land support, who was buying usable institutional buildings, and did that demand fit this property and its market?

Four questions for a thin-evidence appraisal

  1. What use underlies the search? Ask what evidence supports the initial redevelopment, continued-use or conversion assumption.
  2. Who bought the comparable properties? The recorded price alone may not explain what made a building useful to its purchaser.
  3. Does that buyer demand apply here? Test the subject’s permitted uses, layout and local market rather than importing a conclusion from elsewhere.
  4. Why are these sales more relevant than the nearest ones? The report should explain the important differences in time, location and utility.

Re-testing highest and best use may support the original view. The purpose is a supported conclusion, not a higher number or a conversion story for every unusual property.

For an institution preparing a disposition, an owner considering a sale or a lender reviewing a report, the useful starting point is the decision the appraisal must support. Share the planning information and building records available so the appraiser can investigate the property’s actual alternatives.
